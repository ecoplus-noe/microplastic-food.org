In general, microplastics can be analysed in two different ways: (a) non-destructively or on a particle-by-particle basis, that is, using spectroscopic methods (µFTIR and µRaman) to detect and measure microplastic particles down to 10 µm in size. (b) In mass-based methods such as pyrolysis gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (py-GC/MS), the samples are destroyed and the fragment masses are separated chromatographically, allowing both microplastics and nanoplastics to be detected spectroscopically.

With both methods, however, the challenge lies in ensuring the samples are as pure as possible, that is, removing any interfering matrix components such as proteins, fats, or carbohydrates in advance. To achieve this, gentle enzymatic denaturation methods using, for example, proteases, lipases, and amylases must be employed to avoid altering the microplastic particles.

In addition, a (micro)plastic-free laboratory environment (laminar flow) as well as plastic-free equipment and clothing are essential to prevent contamination.

Robustness & Recovery

Already in the first CORNET project, “microplastic@food,” microplastic reference samples were produced that were necessary for the development and calibration of spectroscopic methods (µFTIR and µRaman), for interlaboratory comparisons, and for the development of automated data analysis tools.

Using cryogenic milling, microparticles in three size classes (1000–50 µm, 50–10 µm, and 10–1 µm) were produced from the most common packaging plastics, including PP, PE, PVC, PU, PET, PS, ABS, PA, PC, PMMA, PAN, PLA, PMDS, and PVPP, as well as plastic fibers such as PET and PAN, and the methods were validated.

In the second CORNET project, “MICROPLEXFOOD,” food samples were additionally spiked with artificially produced microplastic particles (spherical, cut, deuterated), and the recovery rate was determined via µFTIR and µRaman spectroscopy in interlaboratory tests.

In all experiments, data analysis was supported by AI-based analysis software.

Standardised Sample Handling

The actual samples analysed were collected on-site by the research team in some cases, but mostly by qualified personnel at food production facilities or by packaging and equipment manufacturers.

To ensure a standardised approach, uniform protocols (Standard Operating Procedures, SOPs) were established for both the food samples and the necessary blank samples taken in the filling environment. These company-provided blank samples, as well as laboratory blank samples, were always processed in parallel with the actual samples, and at least duplicate determinations were performed.

Anonymized Real Food Samples in Case Studies

All real food samples were anonymized, and the results were summarized in case studies. This ensured that no conclusions could be drawn about individual companies.

The results and challenges, as well as measures to prevent microplastic contamination in food, were summarized in guidelines. These are available for download in the News section.
